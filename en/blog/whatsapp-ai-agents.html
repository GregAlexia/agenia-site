AI agents on WhatsApp: what Meta's rules actually allow

AgenIA · 6 min read

Putting an AI agent on WhatsApp is tempting for any small business — it's the channel where customers already read their messages. But WhatsApp isn't a channel like any other: Meta controls who you can contact, when you can write freely, and what an automation is allowed to do on its own. Ignoring that framework doesn't trigger an abstract fine — it gets your business number suspended, often without warning.

What to know

Can you message a prospect first on WhatsApp?

No, with one narrow exception. WhatsApp's Business Messaging Policy is explicit: a business may only contact someone if that person has given it their phone number or username, and has given explicit opt-in permission to receive messages. The business alone is responsible for how it collects that permission and for complying with the law that applies to it — Meta doesn't vet this in advance, it enforces after the fact. Unlike email, there's no WhatsApp equivalent of cold outreach: without consent obtained elsewhere first (your site, a form, in person), there's no legitimate first message to send.

What is the 24-hour customer service window?

Once a customer writes to you, a 24-hour window opens — and resets with every new message from them. Inside that window, the business can reply freely, in text, images or any other format. Once it closes without a new message from the customer, only a message template pre-approved by Meta can be sent. An AI agent can therefore never follow up on its own with a freshly written message once the window has lapsed: outside it, it has to pick from templates that were already approved.

Can an AI agent reply entirely on its own?

It can reply on its own during the 24-hour window, but only under a condition Meta makes mandatory, not optional: any automation used to respond must come with a clear, prompt and direct escalation path to a human. Meta lists the accepted forms: in-chat hand-off to a human agent, a phone number, email, a support form, website support, or an in-store visit. An AI agent that replies in a closed loop, with none of these ways out, breaches the policy even if every answer it gives is correct.

How does Meta charge for messages an AI agent sends?

Since 1 July 2025, Meta has charged per delivered message rather than per conversation — a change that reshuffled every cost model built before that date. For an AI agent a business builds itself (or has built by a provider like AgenIA) that replies inside the 24-hour window, those messages fall under the "Service" category, billed at a fixed rate per market. Since 1 July 2026, Meta has also offered its own native AI agent, billed on a different basis entirely: by token, at $2.00 per million tokens, with a typical message consuming 20,000–25,000 tokens — roughly 4 to 5 US cents per message. Which architecture you choose isn't just a technical decision: it directly changes your cost per message, and the two routes aren't billed on the same basis.

Do you need a technical provider to get access?

Access to the WhatsApp Business Platform (its API, distinct from the consumer app) runs either directly through Meta's Cloud API via a developer account, or through an authorised partner — what Meta now calls a "Solution Provider", formerly a "Business Service Provider" (BSP). Either way, it's the same platform and the same rules: the provider changes the billing and the technical support you get, not the obligations around consent, the window, or escalation.

What else applies, beyond Meta's own rules?

Meta's policy states that the business alone is responsible for securing consents and keeping a published privacy policy up to date, "complying with applicable law" — for a business handling EU customers, that means GDPR, not just WhatsApp's own terms. It also explicitly bars reusing data obtained through a WhatsApp conversation for anything beyond supporting that conversation. The two sets of obligations overlap, but one doesn't substitute for the other: being compliant with Meta doesn't excuse you from checking your legal basis for processing, or from informing the people you message.

AgenIA's take

We're regularly asked to add WhatsApp as a channel to an existing product — it's where customers already read their messages, and wanting to reply there automatically is a natural instinct. But the riskiest reflex we see is designing the automation first, and checking Meta's rules only once the pipeline is already built.

We already apply, elsewhere, the principle Meta mandates for WhatsApp: on Keo, the AI drafts the first reply, the one that has to go out fast, and the rest of the exchange is picked up by the agent — not because Meta's policy requires it on that product today, but because that's where the prospect's trust is actually won, and that part doesn't fully delegate. Extending that same principle to WhatsApp doesn't change the philosophy — it changes where, exactly, the way out to a human has to sit inside the conversation.

Our position on any WhatsApp project we're asked to build: the first question is never "which model", it's "where and how is consent collected, and through which exact path does a customer reach a human". Answering those two questions before writing a single line of code avoids a business number getting suspended three months in — a far more expensive incident than the time spent asking them upfront.

Scope a WhatsApp project before automating it

Thirty minutes is usually enough to check whether your WhatsApp use case holds up against Meta's rules — consent, the 24-hour window, escalation — before you invest in a provider or any development work.
